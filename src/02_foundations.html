Part 1How a price forecast is set up and judged

Before any model, three things decide whether a paper's numbers mean anything: what exactly is being predicted from what, how the data were split, and which error score is reported. All the metrics below are worked on the same six half-hours of a made-up NSW evening so you can check them by hand.

The NEM objects that appear in every paper

RRP (regional reference price)
The spot price, in A$/MWh, set every five minutes for each of the five regions (NSW1, QLD1, VIC1, SA1, TAS1) by AEMO's dispatch engine. Most papers average it to half-hours. It is bounded by a market floor (−A$1,000) and a market price cap (A$17,500 in 2024-25), so "spikes" of A$5,000 to A$17,500 are real, settled prices.
Pre-dispatch (PD)
AEMO's own forward projection of price and demand for every half-hour up to the end of the next trading day, re-published every half-hour from the bids generators have submitted. It is what Sinclair benchmarks against and, crucially, feeds into the model as a known-future input. It is not a statistical forecast: it is the dispatch engine run on current bids, so it changes whenever a generator rebids.
FCAS
Frequency control ancillary services: eight side-markets where a battery is paid to stand ready to inject or absorb power within 6 seconds, 60 seconds or 5 minutes (three "raise" and three "lower" contingency services) plus two regulation services. Li 2024 bids into the six contingency ones alongside the spot market.
Market notices, LOR
Free-text bulletins AEMO publishes when something is happening: lack-of-reserve (LOR1, LOR2, LOR3) warnings, constraint changes, directions. They often precede spikes by hours, which is why Liu 2024 mines them with a language model.
Interconnector flow
MW moving between regions. When a line is at its limit the regions' prices separate, which is one cause of single-region spikes.

The forecasting problem: lags, horizon, covariates

In one sentence. A forecaster is a function from a window of past values (and anything else known now) to one or more future values; the paper's choice of window, horizon and "anything else" is the whole design.

Lags are the past values fed in: "6 lags" means the last six values. The look-back window is how far back they go (Sinclair: 96 half-hours = 48 h). The horizon is how far ahead the output reaches (Sinclair: 32 half-hours = 16 h, scored at 2, 4, 8, 12 and 16 h). A covariate is any input other than the target itself, and the vital distinction is between past-only covariates (yesterday's temperature) and known-future covariates (tomorrow's temperature forecast, AEMO's pre-dispatch price), because only the second kind can tell the model about tomorrow directly.

Time runs left to right. The forecast origin is "now". Everything to the left is available; to the right, only known-future covariates are available. The target row to the right is what gets scored.
Worked example
A model made at 16:00 with a 4-step look-back and a 3-step horizon sees prices at 14:00, 14:30, 15:00, 15:30 (lags), the pre-dispatch prices for 16:30, 17:00, 17:30 (known-future covariates), and must output prices for 16:30, 17:00, 17:30. The 17:30 output is "3 steps ahead" or "1.5 h ahead". Sinclair's "8 h horizon" row is the error of the 16th output step only, not the average of the first sixteen.
Common confusion. "Day-ahead" in Tan 2023 means the 48 half-hours of tomorrow forecast from today; "16 h ahead" in Sinclair means a rolling forecast made every half-hour. They are different tasks with different difficulty, so their MAPEs are not comparable.

Train, validation, test, and walk-forward validation

In one sentence. A model is fitted on the training set, its knobs are chosen on the validation set, and its reported score must come from a test set of later dates it never saw.

With time series the split must be chronological. If random rows are held out, the model has seen the half-hour before and after every test point and the score is meaningless. Walk-forward validation (also "rolling origin") goes further: fit on months 1 to 12, validate on month 13; fit on 1 to 13, validate on 14; and so on, then average. It mimics how the model would actually be used and it exposes a model that only works in one season.

Sinclair's 80:20 split of 49,447 half-hours (37,085 train, 2,440 validation, 9,858 test) and, underneath, the five-fold walk-forward scheme used to pick hyperparameters.
Why it matters here
Tan 2023 tests on four windows of three days each, about 0.7 % of the data, chosen one per season. Ghimire 2024 tests on the single year 2022. Sinclair tests on the last 20 % of Nov 2022 to Aug 2025, roughly Feb to Aug 2025, which includes a volatile winter. A three-day window can be lucky; a whole test year cannot, but a single year can still be atypical.

Look-ahead leakage

In one sentence. Leakage is any way that information from after the forecast origin sneaks into an input, and it produces scores that are too good and impossible to reproduce live.

The two ways it happens in these papers: (1) a transform (wavelet, EMD, scaling) is computed over the whole series at once, so the "past" component at time t was calculated using values after t; (2) a covariate is taken from actual observations instead of the forecast that would have existed at the time (using yesterday's actual temperature for yesterday is fine; using tomorrow's actual temperature for tomorrow is leakage).

A symmetric smoothing filter at time t (the purple window) averages values on both sides of t. Applied to the whole series before splitting, the smoothed "input" at t already contains t+1 and t+2.
Sinclair uses AEMO's forecast weather and pre-dispatch price for the future rows, which are legitimately known at the origin. Ghimire 2024's wavelet pre-processing is the one place on this page where leakage of type (1) is a live question; the abstract does not say whether the transform was applied causally. A 2025 follow-up from a different group makes exactly this criticism of the decomposition-then-forecast pattern.

MAE and RMSE

In one sentence. MAE is the typical size of a miss in dollars; RMSE is the same but squares each miss first, so one big spike miss dominates it.

MAE = mean(|e|) RMSE = sqrt(mean(e²))

Common confusion. A model with low RMSE is not necessarily "better at spikes". RMSE is dominated by spike misses, so a model that never forecasts a spike but sits close to the mean can still have decent RMSE. In one sentence. MAPE divides each miss by that half-hour's actual price before averaging, which makes cheap half-hours count as much as expensive ones and blows up when the price is near zero; nMAPE instead divides the average miss by the average price.

Correlation r and R²

In one sentence. r measures whether forecast and actual go up and down together, on a −1 to 1 scale, and ignores whether the level or the scale is right; R² is r² when the forecast is unbiased and tells you the fraction of variance explained.

KGE: Kling-Gupta efficiency

In one sentence. KGE checks three things at once, whether the forecast tracks the actual (correlation r), whether it has the right amount of variability (ratio of standard deviations, α), and whether it has the right average level (ratio of means, β), and reports how far the triple (r, α, β) is from the perfect (1, 1, 1).

α = sd(ŷ) / sd(y) β = mean(ŷ) / mean(y) KGE = 1 − sqrt((r − 1)² + (α − 1)² + (β − 1)²)

It comes from hydrology, where models that get the average flow right but flatten the floods are a known failure; the same failure happens with price spikes. A value of 1 is perfect; 0 is roughly "as good as forecasting the mean every time"; below about 0.5 is poor. Because α punishes under-dispersion, a forecast that never spikes is penalised even if its MAE is fine.

GPI: global performance indicator, and the other agreement indices

In one sentence. GPI is a home-made composite: take several metrics, rescale each to 0 to 1 across the models being compared, flip the ones where lower is better, and add them up, so it ranks models within one paper and means nothing outside it.

The Ghimire papers also report Willmott's index (WI, 0 to 1, a normalised squared-error score), the Nash-Sutcliffe efficiency (NSE, 1 minus squared error over variance, so 0 means "no better than the mean"), the Legates-McCabe index (same idea with absolute errors, stricter), Theil's U (RMSE relative to the size of the series), and a "promoting percentage" (percent improvement over each baseline). They all move together; the paper's own summary is that TNET was first on all of them at three of four sites.

Worked example of a GPI-style composite
Three models with (MAE, r): A (10, 0.90), B (14, 0.95), C (12, 0.80). Rescale MAE to "lower is better" on 0 to 1: A 1.0, B 0.0, C 0.5. Rescale r: A 0.67, B 1.0, C 0.0. Sum: A 1.67, B 1.0, C 0.5. A wins, but only relative to B and C; add a fourth model and every number changes. That is why the GPI values on Ghimire 2024's card (1.61, 1.33, 1.85, 1.30, 0.78) cannot be compared to anything else.

The Diebold-Mariano test

In one sentence. It asks whether the difference in error between two forecasts is bigger than the noise in that difference, using the fact that errors on consecutive half-hours are correlated.

  1. For each test half-hour t, compute the loss of each forecast, usually |e|, and their difference dt = |eA,t| − |eB,t|.
  2. Take the mean d̄. If A is better, d̄ is negative.
  3. Estimate the standard error of d̄ allowing for autocorrelation (a Newey-West style variance).
  4. DM = d̄ / standard error. Beyond ±1.96 is significant at 5 %; beyond ±2.58 at 1 %.
