Part 1How a price forecast is set up and judged

Before any model, three things decide whether a paper's numbers mean anything: what exactly is being predicted from what, how the data were split, and which error score is reported. All the metrics below are worked on the same six half-hours of a made-up NSW evening so you can check them by hand.

The NEM objects that appear in every paper

RRP (regional reference price)
The spot price, in A$/MWh, set every five minutes for each of the five regions (NSW1, QLD1, VIC1, SA1, TAS1) by AEMO's dispatch engine. Most papers average it to half-hours. It is bounded by a market floor (−A$1,000) and a market price cap (A$17,500 in 2024-25), so "spikes" of A$5,000 to A$17,500 are real, settled prices.
Pre-dispatch (PD)
AEMO's own forward projection of price and demand for every half-hour up to the end of the next trading day, re-published every half-hour from the bids generators have submitted. It is what Sinclair benchmarks against and, crucially, feeds into the model as a known-future input. It is not a statistical forecast: it is the dispatch engine run on current bids, so it changes whenever a generator rebids.
FCAS
Frequency control ancillary services: eight side-markets where a battery is paid to stand ready to inject or absorb power within 6 seconds, 60 seconds or 5 minutes (three "raise" and three "lower" contingency services) plus two regulation services. Li 2024 bids into the six contingency ones alongside the spot market.
Market notices, LOR
Free-text bulletins AEMO publishes when something is happening: lack-of-reserve (LOR1, LOR2, LOR3) warnings, constraint changes, directions. They often precede spikes by hours, which is why Liu 2024 mines them with a language model.
Interconnector flow
MW moving between regions. When a line is at its limit the regions' prices separate, which is one cause of single-region spikes.

The forecasting problem: lags, horizon, covariates

In one sentence. A forecaster is a function from a window of past values (and anything else known now) to one or more future values; the paper's choice of window, horizon and "anything else" is the whole design.

Lags are the past values fed in: "6 lags" means the last six values. The look-back window is how far back they go (Sinclair: 96 half-hours = 48 h). The horizon is how far ahead the output reaches (Sinclair: 32 half-hours = 16 h, scored at 2, 4, 8, 12 and 16 h). A covariate is any input other than the target itself, and the vital distinction is between past-only covariates (yesterday's temperature) and known-future covariates (tomorrow's temperature forecast, AEMO's pre-dispatch price), because only the second kind can tell the model about tomorrow directly.

Time runs left to right. The forecast origin is "now". Everything to the left is available; to the right, only known-future covariates are available. The target row to the right is what gets scored.
Worked example
A model made at 16:00 with a 4-step look-back and a 3-step horizon sees prices at 14:00, 14:30, 15:00, 15:30 (lags), the pre-dispatch prices for 16:30, 17:00, 17:30 (known-future covariates), and must output prices for 16:30, 17:00, 17:30. The 17:30 output is "3 steps ahead" or "1.5 h ahead". Sinclair's "8 h horizon" row is the error of the 16th output step only, not the average of the first sixteen.
Common confusion. "Day-ahead" in Tan 2023 means the 48 half-hours of tomorrow forecast from today; "16 h ahead" in Sinclair means a rolling forecast made every half-hour. They are different tasks with different difficulty, so their MAPEs are not comparable.

Train, validation, test, and walk-forward validation

In one sentence. A model is fitted on the training set, its knobs are chosen on the validation set, and its reported score must come from a test set of later dates it never saw.

With time series the split must be chronological. If random rows are held out, the model has seen the half-hour before and after every test point and the score is meaningless. Walk-forward validation (also "rolling origin") goes further: fit on months 1 to 12, validate on month 13; fit on 1 to 13, validate on 14; and so on, then average. It mimics how the model would actually be used and it exposes a model that only works in one season.

Sinclair's 80:20 split of 49,447 half-hours (37,085 train, 2,440 validation, 9,858 test) and, underneath, the five-fold walk-forward scheme used to pick hyperparameters.
Why it matters here
Tan 2023 tests on four windows of three days each, about 0.7 % of the data, chosen one per season. Ghimire 2024 tests on the single year 2022. Sinclair tests on the last 20 % of Nov 2022 to Aug 2025, roughly Feb to Aug 2025, which includes a volatile winter. A three-day window can be lucky; a whole test year cannot, but a single year can still be atypical.

Look-ahead leakage

In one sentence. Leakage is any way that information from after the forecast origin sneaks into an input, and it produces scores that are too good and impossible to reproduce live.

The two ways it happens in these papers: (1) a transform (wavelet, EMD, scaling) is computed over the whole series at once, so the "past" component at time t was calculated using values after t; (2) a covariate is taken from actual observations instead of the forecast that would have existed at the time (using yesterday's actual temperature for yesterday is fine; using tomorrow's actual temperature for tomorrow is leakage).

A symmetric smoothing filter at time t (the purple window) averages values on both sides of t. Applied to the whole series before splitting, the smoothed "input" at t already contains t+1 and t+2.
Sinclair uses AEMO's forecast weather and pre-dispatch price for the future rows, which are legitimately known at the origin. Ghimire 2024's wavelet pre-processing is the one place on this page where leakage of type (1) is a live question; the abstract does not say whether the transform was applied causally. A 2025 follow-up from a different group makes exactly this criticism of the decomposition-then-forecast pattern.

KGE: Kling-Gupta efficiency

In one sentence. KGE checks three things at once, whether the forecast tracks the actual (correlation r), whether it has the right amount of variability (ratio of standard deviations, α), and whether it has the right average level (ratio of means, β), and reports how far the triple (r, α, β) is from the perfect (1, 1, 1).

α = sd(ŷ) / sd(y) β = mean(ŷ) / mean(y) KGE = 1 − sqrt((r − 1)² + (α − 1)² + (β − 1)²)

It comes from hydrology, where models that get the average flow right but flatten the floods are a known failure; the same failure happens with price spikes. A value of 1 is perfect; 0 is roughly "as good as forecasting the mean every time"; below about 0.5 is poor. Because α punishes under-dispersion, a forecast that never spikes is penalised even if its MAE is fine.

GPI: global performance indicator, and the other agreement indices

In one sentence. GPI is a home-made composite: take several metrics, rescale each to 0 to 1 across the models being compared, flip the ones where lower is better, and add them up, so it ranks models within one paper and means nothing outside it.

The Ghimire papers also report Willmott's index (WI, 0 to 1, a normalised squared-error score), the Nash-Sutcliffe efficiency (NSE, 1 minus squared error over variance, so 0 means "no better than the mean"), the Legates-McCabe index (same idea with absolute errors, stricter), Theil's U (RMSE relative to the size of the series), and a "promoting percentage" (percent improvement over each baseline). They all move together; the paper's own summary is that TNET was first on all of them at three of four sites.

Worked example of a GPI-style composite

A wins, but only relative to B and C; add a fourth model and every number changes. That is why the GPI values on Ghimire 2024's card (1.61, 1.33, 1.85, 1.30, 0.78) cannot be compared to anything else.

The Diebold-Mariano test

In one sentence. It asks whether the difference in error between two forecasts is bigger than the noise in that difference, using the fact that errors on consecutive half-hours are correlated.

  1. For each test half-hour t, compute the loss of each forecast, usually |e|, and their difference dt = |eA,t| − |eB,t|.
  2. Take the mean d̄. If A is better, d̄ is negative.
  3. Estimate the standard error of d̄ allowing for autocorrelation (a Newey-West style variance).
  4. DM = d̄ / standard error. Beyond ±1.96 is significant at 5 %; beyond ±2.58 at 1 %.

Part 2Building blocks: the networks, the trees, and the pre-processing tricks

Every model in the papers is assembled from the pieces below. The pieces are grouped as: how a network is trained at all; the three classic shapes (dense, convolutional, recurrent); the odd ones (autoencoders, random-weight networks); the non-neural baselines; the hyperparameter search; and the "decompose first" pre-processing family.

A neuron, a layer, a dense network (MLP, DNN, BPNN)

In one sentence. A neuron multiplies each input by a weight, adds them up with a bias, and passes the sum through a simple bend (an activation function); a layer is many neurons on the same inputs; a dense network stacks layers so bends compose into any curve.

Training: loss, gradient descent, epochs, learning rate, Adam

In one sentence. Training measures how wrong the network is with a loss function, computes which direction each weight should move to reduce it (the gradient, by back-propagation), and takes a small step that way, over and over.

One pass through the training set is an epoch; Sinclair trains for up to 20. The learning rate is the step size (2 × 10−4 in Sinclair): too big and training bounces, too small and it crawls. Adam is the standard optimiser; it keeps a running average of past gradients so each weight gets its own adaptive step. Batch size (128 in Sinclair) is how many samples are averaged per step. Reduce-on-plateau halves the learning rate when validation loss stops improving; early stopping ends training when it has not improved for several epochs (patience 5 in Sinclair) to avoid overfitting.

Loss as a landscape over one weight. Each step moves downhill by learning-rate × slope. Adam scales the step per weight.

Loss functions: MSE, MAE, Huber, and quantile (pinball)

In one sentence. The loss decides what "wrong" costs; squared error makes the model chase spikes hard, absolute error makes it aim for the median and ignore them, Huber is squared near zero and absolute far away, and quantile loss tilts the penalty so the model learns a specific percentile.

Loss against the size of the miss. Huber (δ = 0.8 in Sinclair, on scaled data) follows MSE inside ±δ and MAE outside, so a A$10,000 spike miss does not dominate training the way it would under MSE.

Overfitting and the standard defences: dropout, layer normalisation, residual connections

In one sentence. Overfitting is memorising the training set's noise; dropout randomly switches neurons off during training so no single path can be relied on, layer normalisation rescales each sample's activations so training is stable, and a residual connection adds a layer's input straight to its output so the layer only has to learn a correction.

Left: dropout knocks out a random 5 % (Sinclair) to 30 % of units each step. Middle: LayerNorm subtracts the mean and divides by the standard deviation across a sample's features. Right: the residual "skip" wire around a block; "pre-LN" (Sinclair's Figure 3) normalises before the block instead of after, which trains more stably in deep stacks.
Common confusion. Layer normalisation normalises across the features of one sample. Batch normalisation normalises each feature across the samples in a batch. Transformers use the first because sequences in a batch have different lengths and statistics.

Scaling inputs: standard, robust, quantile, and RevIN

In one sentence. Networks train badly on raw dollars, so every input is rescaled to a similar range; the choice matters for a series with a A$−1,000 to A$17,500 range and a median around A$100.

Standard scaling subtracts the mean and divides by the standard deviation; one spike inflates the standard deviation and squashes all the ordinary prices into a narrow band. Robust scaling uses the median and inter-quartile range instead. Quantile transformer scaling (Sinclair's choice) maps every value to its rank on a 0 to 1 scale and then to a normal distribution, so A$100 and A$10,000 land at their percentiles and the spike stops dominating. RevIN (reversible instance normalisation, used inside PatchTST) normalises each input window by its own mean and standard deviation and un-does it on the output, so the model sees "shape" not "level".

1-D convolutional network (CNN)

In one sentence. A small window of weights (a kernel or filter) slides along the series and at each position outputs the weighted sum of the values under it, so the same pattern detector is applied everywhere; many filters run in parallel, and stacking layers detects patterns of patterns.

Terms you will meet: kernel size (how many time steps a filter spans), stride (how far it jumps each move), padding (zeros added at the ends so the output is the same length), channels (number of filters, or number of input series), pooling (take the max or mean over small blocks to shorten the series and keep the strongest response), Conv1D (Keras's name for this layer). A TCN (temporal convolutional network) is a stack of causal, dilated 1-D convolutions that only look backwards; the companion page walks one through numerically.

The kernel (−1, 0, +1) slid along eight half-hourly prices. Each output is "price two steps later minus price two steps earlier": a ramp detector. ReLU then keeps only the up-ramps.

Recurrent networks: RNN, LSTM, GRU, BiLSTM

In one sentence. A recurrent network reads the series one step at a time and carries a vector of memory forward, updating it at each step with the new value; an LSTM adds gates that learn what to keep, what to forget and what to output, and a GRU is a lighter version with two gates.

The basic RNN update is ht = tanh(W·xt + U·ht−1 + b): the new memory is a bend of "this input plus the old memory". Its weakness is that the same U is multiplied in at every step, so signals from 50 steps back either vanish or explode. The LSTM fixes this with a separate cell state ct that is edited additively: a forget gate f (0 to 1) scales the old cell, an input gate i scales a proposed new value c̃, and an output gate o decides how much of the cell is exposed as ht. The GRU merges cell and hidden state and uses a reset gate and an update gate. A bidirectional LSTM runs a second copy from the end of the window backwards and concatenates the two memories, which is only legitimate when the whole window is in the past.

Autoencoders: plain, denoising, sparse, stacked (SDAE, SSDAE)

In one sentence. An autoencoder is trained to output its own input through a narrower middle layer, so the middle layer is forced to become a compressed summary; variants change what goes in (noise added) or what the middle is allowed to do (mostly off).

  1. Plain. Encoder squeezes 48 half-hours to, say, 8 numbers; decoder expands back to 48; loss is reconstruction error. The 8 numbers are features.
  2. Denoising (DAE). Random noise (or random zeros) is added to the input, the target is still the clean input. The network can only succeed by learning the structure that survives noise, so its features ignore bid-by-bid jitter.
  3. Sparse. A penalty keeps the average activation of each middle neuron near a small value like 0.05, so on any given input most are off and each active one stands for something specific.
  4. Stacked (SDAE, SSDAE). Train one, freeze it, train a second on the first's middle layer, and so on; then put a regression layer on top and fine-tune the whole stack. Faster and more stable than training a deep network from scratch, which was the point in 2023.
Noisy in, clean out, through a bottleneck. Grey bottleneck units are the ones the sparsity penalty has switched off for this input.

Common confusion. "Encoder" in "autoencoder" and "encoder" in "transformer encoder" are different things. The first compresses to a bottleneck; the second is a stack of attention layers that keeps the sequence length.

The non-neural baselines: random forest, XGBoost, SVM, ELM

In one sentence. These are the "conventional machine learning" rows in the tables: decision-tree ensembles that split on thresholds, a kernel regression that fits a tube, and a random-feature network.

One regression tree on two features. A random forest averages hundreds of such trees, each grown on a random subset of rows and features. XGBoost grows them one at a time, each fitted to the errors the previous ones left.
  • Random forest (RF). Many trees, each on a bootstrap sample; prediction is the average. Robust, no scaling needed, cannot extrapolate above the largest training price.
  • XGBoost (XGB). Gradient boosting: tree 1 fits the target, tree 2 fits tree 1's residuals, and so on, with regularisation. Usually the strongest tabular baseline; same extrapolation limit.
  • Support vector machine / regression (SVM). Fits the flattest function that keeps most points inside a tube of width ε, using a kernel to allow curves. Scales badly beyond tens of thousands of rows.
  • Extreme learning machine (ELM). A one-hidden-layer network whose hidden weights are random and fixed; only the output weights are fitted by least squares. The RVFL below is an ELM with an added direct input-to-output link.

Random vector functional link network (RVFL)

In one sentence. Take the inputs, push them through a hidden layer whose weights were drawn at random and are never changed, then fit a single linear regression from [inputs, hidden outputs] to the target by least squares; the randomness supplies nonlinearity for free and the least-squares fit takes milliseconds.

"Functional link" is the direct wire from the inputs to the output, so the model is "ordinary linear regression plus random nonlinear extra features". Least squares means solving for the output weights β that minimise Σ(y − Hβ)², which has the closed-form answer β = (HᵀH + λI)−1Hᵀy (λ is a small ridge penalty). No gradient descent, no epochs, no learning rate. In Ghimire 2024 the inputs to the RVFL are the CNN's features, so the "C" in CRVFL is the CNN and the RVFL is the regression head.

Hyperparameter search: grid, random, and Bayesian optimisation

In one sentence. Hyperparameters are the settings you choose rather than train (lags, layers, learning rate, wavelet level); Bayesian optimisation picks the next setting to try by fitting a probabilistic curve to the trials so far and going where the predicted score is high or the uncertainty is wide.

Grid search tries every combination (3 lags × 4 levels × 5 filter counts = 60 trainings). Random search samples combinations at random and is surprisingly competitive because usually only one or two settings matter. Bayesian optimisation keeps a surrogate model, almost always a Gaussian process, which gives a mean and a standard deviation for the score at every untried setting, and an acquisition function such as expected improvement that trades off exploiting good regions against exploring uncertain ones. Typically 20 to 50 trials replace hundreds.

Empirical mode decomposition: EMD, EEMD, CEEMDAN, ICEEMDAN

In one sentence. EMD peels a series into a handful of oscillations from fastest to slowest (intrinsic mode functions, IMFs) plus a slow residual, using only the series' own peaks and troughs; the "ensemble" and "adaptive noise" variants fix its habit of mixing frequencies by averaging over many noisy copies.

  1. Find all local maxima and all local minima of the series.
  2. Draw a smooth curve (a cubic spline) through the maxima, another through the minima: the upper and lower envelopes.
  3. Subtract the average of the two envelopes from the series. Repeat ("sift") on the result until what is left oscillates around zero with one peak per zero-crossing. That is IMF1, the fastest component.
  4. Subtract IMF1 from the original and repeat from step 1 to get IMF2, and so on, until the residual has no more oscillations.

Mode mixing: plain EMD often puts two different frequencies in one IMF or the same frequency across two. EEMD adds white noise to the series, runs EMD, repeats a few hundred times with different noise and averages the IMFs; the noise fills in the gaps so components separate cleanly. CEEMDAN adds the noise stage by stage so the IMFs sum back exactly to the series; ICEEMDAN ("improved") adds noise as the EMD of noise rather than raw noise, which removes spurious early modes and leaves less noise in the result. Then each IMF is forecast separately and the forecasts are summed.

Illustrative (a synthetic series, not real sifting): an evening price shape decomposes into bid noise, a daily cycle, and a trend. The slow parts are easy to forecast; the error concentrates in IMF1.
Common confusion. EMD is not a fixed filter; the components depend on the whole series, so an IMF at time t computed on the full dataset has seen the future (see leakage). A causal use must recompute the decomposition on the history available at each origin, which is expensive and is why the review of Tan's paper notes the three-day test windows.

Wavelet transforms: DWT and MoDWT, levels, approximation and detail

In one sentence. A wavelet transform splits a series into a smooth "approximation" and a set of "detail" series, one per scale, using fixed short filters; the ordinary DWT halves the length at each level, while the maximal-overlap DWT (MoDWT) applies the same filters at every time step so every level stays full length and lined up with the original.

The simplest wavelet, Haar, uses the filters "average of two neighbours" (low-pass, gives the approximation A) and "half their difference" (high-pass, gives the detail D). Level 1 applies them to the series; level 2 applies them again to A1; each level doubles the time scale it captures. The number of levels is a hyperparameter (Ghimire chose it by Bayesian optimisation). Longer wavelets (Daubechies db4, symlets) use 4 to 8 coefficients instead of 2 and give smoother components. In the MoDWT the filters are applied at every t instead of every second t, so no information is thrown away and the components can be fed as ordinary aligned input columns.

Other decompositions that appear as baselines: SSA, VMD, wavelet-vs-EMD

Singular spectrum analysis (SSA) stacks lagged copies of the series into a matrix, takes its singular value decomposition, and rebuilds the series from the top few components: trend, oscillations, noise. Variational mode decomposition (VMD) solves an optimisation for a fixed number K of band-limited modes with centre frequencies; it is less prone to mode mixing than EMD but you must choose K. Tan 2023 tried wavelet (WT), SSA and EEMD in front of the same CNN+SSDAE and found ICEEMDAN best; the review's table shows wavelet actually made that model worse than no decomposition.

The "decompose, forecast each part, add up" pattern

In one sentence. Split the series into components that are individually smoother than the whole, forecast each with its own model, and sum the forecasts; it works because the summed error is usually less than the error of forecasting the raw series, and it fails when the decomposition itself needs the future.

The template behind Tan 2023 (ICEEMDAN in front, CNN+SSDAE per component), Ghimire 2024 (MoDWT in front, one CNN-RVFL on all components), and a dozen NEM papers not on this page.
Reading the results tables with this in mind
In Tan's table the raw-price CNN+SSDAE has MAPE 8.4 to 9.7 % and the ICEEMDAN version 6.0 to 7.0 %, so the decomposition is worth about 2.5 points. Part 3Attention and the transformer family

A recurrent network reaches the past through a chain of memory updates. Attention reaches it directly: every output position computes a weight for every input position and takes the weighted average. Everything in this part is a variation on that one operation.

Softmax: turning scores into weights that sum to 1

In one sentence. Softmax exponentiates each score and divides by the total, so bigger scores get disproportionately bigger shares and the shares add to exactly 1.

softmax(s)i = exp(si) / Σj exp(sj)

That is why attention divides scores by √d (the vector length): to keep them from getting so large that the softmax becomes a hard argmax with no gradient.

Attention: queries, keys, values, and heads

In one sentence. Each position produces three vectors from its input, a query (what am I looking for), a key (what do I contain) and a value (what I hand over); an output position scores every input by query·key, softmaxes the scores into weights, and outputs the weighted sum of the values.

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(Q Kᵀ / √d) V

Self-attention: queries, keys and values all come from the same sequence (the past looks at the past). Cross-attention: queries come from one sequence (the future steps) and keys/values from another (the encoded past). Multi-head: run h copies with different learned projections and concatenate; each head can specialise (one on "same time yesterday", one on "last two hours"). Causal mask: in decoder self-attention, position t is forbidden from attending to positions after t, so an autoregressive model cannot peek ahead.

Positional encoding and Time2Vec

In one sentence. Attention treats its inputs as a bag with no order, so each position gets a vector added to it that encodes where (or when) it is; the classic recipe is a set of sines and cosines at different frequencies, and Time2Vec is the same idea with the frequencies learned.

The sinusoidal encoding for position p uses sin(p / 100002i/d) and cos(...) for i = 0 … d/2, so low dimensions oscillate fast (distinguish neighbours) and high dimensions slowly (distinguish far apart). Sinclair adds this to both encoder and decoder inputs and additionally feeds explicit calendar features (sine and cosine of half-hour-of-day, day-of-week, month) so the model knows the time of day directly, not just the position in the window. Time2Vec (Ghimire 2023) replaces the fixed table with one linear term plus k learned sinusoids, t2v(τ) = [ω₀τ + φ₀, sin(ω₁τ + φ₁), …], so the network can discover a daily and a weekly frequency itself.

Three of the sinusoidal channels over 48 positions, and the daily sine/cosine pair that Sinclair uses so that 23:30 and 00:00 are near each other.

One transformer layer, and a stack of them

In one sentence. A layer is multi-head attention, then a small two-layer feed-forward network applied to each position separately, each wrapped in a residual connection and a layer normalisation; a transformer is N of these in a row.

The sizes you see in Sinclair's Table 2: d_model = 128 is the width of every position's vector; heads = 4 means four 32-wide attention heads; ff_dim = 512 is the hidden width of the per-position feed-forward network; layers = 3 is the stack depth; dropout = 0.05. "Tiny" to "large" scale these from 64/2/256/2 up to 512/8/2048/4, and the small one won, which the paper attributes to the dataset's size (about 37,000 training windows).

Pre-LayerNorm layout (Sinclair's Figure 3): normalise, attend, add the skip; normalise, feed-forward, add the skip. The feed-forward part is where most of the parameters live.

Encoder-decoder, encoder-only, decoder-only; parallel versus autoregressive decoding

In one sentence. The encoder is a transformer over the past; the decoder is a transformer over the future positions that also cross-attends to the encoder's output; you can keep both, only the encoder (then regress the future from a pooled or flattened past), or only the decoder (then the future positions attend only to themselves and their known-future covariates).

The three shapes. In decoder-only, the only information about the past is whatever is inside the known-future covariates (for the NEM: AEMO's pre-dispatch, which was itself computed from the current state of the system).

Parallel (non-autoregressive) decoding: the decoder is given all 32 future covariate rows at once and emits all 32 prices in one forward pass. Autoregressive decoding: emit step 1, append it to the decoder input, emit step 2, and so on, 32 passes; during training the true previous values are fed instead of the predicted ones ("teacher forcing"), which is a mismatch with how it runs live and one reason Sinclair's autoregressive variants were 2 to 4 points worse and slower.

Reading Sinclair's five variants
(a) enc-dec parallel, (b) enc-dec autoregressive, (c) encoder-only, (d) decoder-only parallel, (e) decoder-only autoregressive. NSW 2 h nMAPE: 37.9, 40.8, 42.7, 33.6, 38.7. The two facts to take away: dropping the encoder (c → d comparison is the wrong one; compare a → d) improved the score, and dropping the future covariates (c) cost 5 to 9 points. History is nearly redundant once you have pre-dispatch.
Common confusion. "Decoder-only" here is not the GPT sense of "predict the next token from previous tokens". Sinclair's decoder-only model has no autoregression in variant (d); it is a transformer over 32 rows of forecast covariates that outputs 32 prices. The name refers to which half of the original architecture is kept.

Known-future covariates: why the pre-dispatch column decides everything

In one sentence. A covariate that is known for the forecast horizon (a weather forecast, a calendar flag, AEMO's pre-dispatch) can be handed to the decoder row by row, and any model that can accept such rows will beat any model that cannot, whatever its architecture.

Sinclair's SHAP result: the pre-dispatch NSW price contributes more than 60 % of total feature importance; forecast demand, interconnector flows and other regions' pre-dispatch prices come next; the 48 hours of history contribute "comparatively little". PatchTST and TimesFM have no way to accept the right-hand columns, and they score 57 to 116 % nMAPE against 34 to 44 % for everything that can.

Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT)

In one sentence. TFT is a forecasting-specific design that learns a weight for every input variable (variable selection), runs an LSTM over past and future rows for local order, applies one interpretable attention layer for long-range structure, gates any block that is not helping, and outputs quantiles.

  1. Variable selection network. For each time step, a softmax over the input variables gives each a weight; the weighted sum is what the rest of the model sees. The weights are a built-in importance report.
  2. LSTM encoder-decoder. Reads the past rows, then the known-future rows, producing a local context per step.
  3. Gated residual network (GRN). Every block is wrapped in a learned gate that can pass its input straight through; the model can effectively turn off the attention layer if the LSTM alone suffices.
  4. Interpretable multi-head attention. One attention layer over the whole window, with the heads' value projections shared so their weights can be averaged and read.
  5. Quantile outputs. Trained with the pinball loss at q = 0.1, 0.5, 0.9, so it emits a band.
TFT's data path. It handles static, past-only and known-future inputs separately, which is why it is the natural comparison model for the NEM problem.
Where it landed
TFT was within 1 to 3 points of the best transformer in NSW and QLD and was the best model in VIC (36.3 to 39.6 %). Its 0.5-quantile is what the tables score, so it competes on a point forecast while also giving the band the others lack.

PatchTST: patches and channel independence

In one sentence. Cut the input window into overlapping patches of P steps with stride S, treat each patch as one token, run an encoder-only transformer over the tokens, and do this separately for each input series; a linear layer maps the encoded tokens to the horizon.

Patching. Attention runs over 13 patch-tokens instead of 96 step-tokens, so it is cheaper and each token carries a local shape.

Informer, ProbSparse attention, and the "Graph Patch Informer"

In one sentence. Informer is a long-horizon encoder-decoder transformer that makes attention cheap by only computing the full weight row for the few queries whose attention would be most peaked (ProbSparse), and shrinks the sequence between encoder layers (distilling); Malyala's GPI adds patching in front and a graph layer over the patches.

The sparsity trick: for each query, measure how far its score distribution is from uniform (a cheap approximation of KL divergence using a sample of keys). Queries that would attend almost uniformly contribute nothing useful, so give them the mean of the values instead of computing their row. Only the top u = c·ln(L) queries get real attention. Cost falls from L² to L·ln L.

Graph neural network (GNN), as used in "Graph Patch Informer" and in Han & Wang's PJM paper
A graph has nodes (say, the five NEM regions, or the patches of a window) and edges (interconnectors, or learned similarities). One GNN layer updates each node by combining its own vector with an aggregate of its neighbours' vectors, then applying a small network. Worked step with three regions and a simple mean aggregation: NSW = 100, QLD = 80, VIC = 120, edges NSW–QLD and NSW–VIC. New NSW = f(100, mean(80, 120)) = f(100, 100); new QLD = f(80, 100); new VIC = f(120, 100). Two layers let VIC's information reach QLD via NSW. In Malyala's model the exact graph is not described in the abstract; the caption-level evidence says it sits between the patches and the Informer attention.

TimesFM: a time-series foundation model, used zero-shot

In one sentence. TimesFM is a decoder-only transformer that Google pre-trained on a very large collection of real and synthetic time series so that, given any new history, it produces a forecast without any training on that data.

Internally it patches the input (32 steps per input patch), predicts a longer output patch (128 steps) per step, and was trained with a causal mask so that any prefix of the context works. It has a context limit (512 to 2048 steps depending on version) and takes only the target series, no covariates. "Zero-shot" means exactly that: Sinclair did not fine-tune it and fed it only the price. Fine-tuning would mean continuing its training on NEM data; a covariate-aware version exists but was not used.

Pre-train once on everything; then apply to any series. The trade-off is generality against the inability to use what makes the NEM predictable (pre-dispatch).
Where it landed
NSW nMAPE 48 to 60 %: better than AEMO beyond 4 h, worse than every trained model. Note the spike/non-spike split: on spike windows TimesFM's 2 h score (73.1) was the second best of all models, because when a spike is already under way in the context it extrapolates it, whereas the covariate models were pulled back toward pre-dispatch.

Large language models, fine-tuning, LoRA and QLoRA

In one sentence. An LLM is a very large decoder-only transformer trained to predict the next text token; fine-tuning continues that training on your task; LoRA makes fine-tuning cheap by freezing the model and training small low-rank add-on matrices; QLoRA additionally stores the frozen model in 4-bit precision so it fits on one GPU.

A token is a word piece (roughly ¾ of a word). "Generative pre-trained" means trained on the web to continue text. In Liu 2024 an open-access model (the paper's index terms mention no specific one) is fine-tuned to read an AEMO notice and emit a structured record. QLoRA: keep W in 4-bit "NormalFloat", de-quantise on the fly, train the LoRA adapters in 16-bit.

Part 4Beyond point forecasting: state-space models, reinforcement learning, and explaining a model

State-space models and the GRU-based DSSM

In one sentence. A state-space model says there is a hidden state that evolves step to step and the thing you observe is a noisy function of that state; a deep state-space model lets a network (here a GRU) learn the evolution, and Malyala's DSSM ties the hidden state to aggregate demand.

statet = F(statet−1, inputt) + noise pricet = G(statet) + noise

The classical version (Kalman filter) has F and G linear. The deep version makes F a GRU update and G a small network. The design choice in DSSM is to make the state mean something: by training it to reproduce demand as well as price, the state becomes an estimate of system load, and the output layer learns the demand-to-price curve. That is a supply-curve model in disguise: price steps up when the state crosses the level where more expensive plant is needed.

Hidden state evolving under the GRU; each observation is read off through G. The dotted supervision arrow is what makes the state resemble demand.
Why "no weather" is a coherent choice
Weather matters to price only through demand and rooftop solar. If the state is demand and the model has demand history, the weather is already inside the state. What it cannot do is anticipate a demand change that has not started yet (tomorrow's heatwave); that is precisely what a weather forecast covariate adds in Sinclair's model.

Reinforcement learning, actor-critic, and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC)

In one sentence. Instead of predicting a price and then optimising against it, a reinforcement-learning agent maps what it sees (state) directly to what it does (action) and is trained by the money it makes (reward); the actor is the network that chooses actions, the critic is the network that estimates how much future reward an action leads to, and SAC adds a bonus for keeping the actor's choices random enough to keep exploring.

State
In Li 2024: the 64-number summary of the last 160 minutes of seven prices (from the transformer) plus the battery's state of charge.
Action
Continuous: charge/discharge power for the spot market and a bid quantity for each of the six FCAS markets.
Reward
Spot revenue (discharge MW × price, negative when charging) plus FCAS availability payments for the interval.
Policy π(a | s)
The actor: a network outputting a distribution over actions; SAC samples from it.
Q(s, a)
The critic: expected discounted future reward from taking a in s. Discount γ = 0.99 means reward n steps ahead counts 0.99n.
Entropy bonus
SAC maximises reward + α × entropy of π, so the actor stays stochastic and does not lock onto one habit before it has seen enough spikes.
The loop. The transformer's weights are updated by the same gradient as the actor's, so it learns whatever price features make money, not whatever features predict price.
Worked example: why forecast-then-optimise can lose to an agent
A 1 MWh battery, three intervals, actual prices . Perfect-information optimisation (PIO): charge at , discharge at , revenue A$. A forecaster that predicted would tell the optimiser to charge at interval 2 and discharge at interval 3 for a predicted A$; at the real prices that buys at A$300 and sells at A$40, earning A$. An agent trained on many such days learns "when the last 160 minutes look like this, discharge in the next interval" without ever committing to a price number, and Li's Table II shows this gained 15 to 32 % over the transformer-forecast-then-optimise benchmark on the same data. The catch: the agent has no guarantee, only a track record.

The benchmarks in Li's table. MILP (mixed-integer linear programming) is the exact optimiser used by LP&O, TP&O and PIO: given a price path, it solves for the charge/discharge schedule that maximises revenue subject to the battery's power, energy and efficiency limits. LP&O feeds it an LSTM's forecast, TP&O a transformer's forecast, PIO the actual prices (an upper bound no live strategy can reach). MLP-DRL is the same SAC agent with a plain dense network instead of the transformer summarising the price history.

SHAP: attributing a prediction to its inputs

In one sentence. SHAP splits the difference between a model's prediction and its average prediction into one share per input, where each share is the input's average marginal contribution over every possible order of adding the inputs (the Shapley value from game theory).

Worked example with two inputs, by hand
Model output for a half-hour: with no inputs (the average prediction) A$100; with only the pre-dispatch price known, A$180; with only the weather forecast known, A$110; with both, A$200.
Order "pre-dispatch first, then weather": pre-dispatch adds 180 − 100 = 80, weather adds 200 − 180 = 20.
Order "weather first, then pre-dispatch": weather adds 110 − 100 = 10, pre-dispatch adds 200 − 110 = 90.
Shapley value = average over orders: pre-dispatch = (80 + 90) / 2 = 85, weather = (20 + 10) / 2 = 15. They add to 100 = 200 − 100, as they must. Feature importance for the whole test set is the mean of |SHAP| over all half-hours; that is Sinclair's Figure 8, where the pre-dispatch NSW price alone has more than 60 % of the total.

Walk one day through it

Reinforcement learning loop: the agent acts, the market simulator pays it, and the payment trains both the actor and the transformer end to end. Nothing is ever trained to "predict the price".

Walk one interval through it
